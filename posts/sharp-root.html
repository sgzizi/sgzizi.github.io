When can a random polynomial put a root in a short interval?

Zijun Wang · Learning theory · 4 min read

A worked explanation of root anti-concentration: dependence counterexamples, ordered-root densities, a short proof, and singular coefficient laws.

Read the paper Preprint

In this article

  1. A local probability question
  2. Why coefficient marginals are insufficient
  3. The ordered-root criterion
  4. A singular law that still works
  5. Beyond polynomials

A local probability question

Suppose an algorithm changes its behaviour whenever a polynomial in a tuning parameter becomes zero. Even a low-degree polynomial can cause unstable behaviour if its random roots concentrate in a very short interval.

Our preprint studies the probability of at least one real root falling in an interval I. For a monic degree-d polynomial with coefficient law μ, define the interval-hitting constant:

cμ(Θ) = supI ⊆ Θ Pr{P has a root in I} / |I|

The supremum is over bounded, nondegenerate closed intervals. A finite constant means that shrinking an interval forces its hit probability to shrink at least linearly in its length.

The distinction between the number of roots and their location is essential. Degree bounds how many roots a polynomial can have. It does not stop one of those roots from being deterministic.

Why coefficient marginals are insufficient

Let U be uniform on [−1, 1], and consider:

PU(x) = x² + Ux − 1 − U = (x − 1)(x + 1 + U)

Both random coefficients have bounded marginal densities. Nevertheless, x = 1 is a root for every draw. Any interval containing 1 has hit probability one, however short it is.

The failure comes from dependence: the coefficients move together in a way that pins a root. Separate histograms of the coefficients would miss that constraint.

This example motivates a condition stated directly in terms of where roots land. It also explains why replacing a joint-density assumption with marginal-density bounds is unsafe.

Counterexample and coefficient-space conditions: Section 3.1.

The ordered-root criterion

For each polynomial, list its distinct real roots in Θ in increasing order. The kth root may not exist, so its law is a subprobability measure: it records a location only on draws with at least k roots.

Let M be the largest essential density bound among those d ordered-root laws. Theorem 3.2 proves:

M ≤ cμ(Θ) ≤ dM

The upper bound is a union bound

If some root hits I, at least one ordered root hits I. Each ordered-root probability is at most M|I|. Summing at most d such probabilities gives:

Pr{some root ∈ I} ≤ Σk=1…d Pr{kth root exists and lies in I} ≤ dM|I|

The converse uses event containment

The event that a particular ordered root lies in I is contained in the event that some root lies in I. A finite hitting constant therefore bounds each ordered-root measure on every interval.

Extending this interval domination to Borel sets yields absolute continuity and a bounded density. That is the measure-theoretic step behind the converse; the event inclusion alone is only the beginning.

The result holds even when the coefficient law is singular. Proposition 3.3 also shows that the factor d is sharp in general, including among laws with bounded coefficient densities.

Precise statement, measurability, and sharpness: Theorem 3.2 and Proposition 3.3.

A singular law that still works

For an illustrative calculation on the real line, fix distinct numbers b₁ < ⋯ < bd, take d ≥ 2, and draw a shared offset Z uniformly on [−a, a], with a > 0.

PZ(x) = ∏j=1…d (x − bj − Z)

Every root is bj + Z, with density at most 1/(2a). Thus the hit probability is at most min{1, d|I|/(2a)}.

Yet the d-dimensional coefficient vector is generated by one scalar. Its law lies on a curve and has no full-dimensional Lebesgue density. The root bound survives because the roots themselves move continuously with the offset.

Compare this with the previous example. Both have strongly dependent coefficients. One pins a root; the other translates all roots. Singularity of the coefficient law does not settle the anti-concentration question.

This calculation illustrates the rigid-translation mechanism used in the paper’s common-offset polynomial-kernel application. The application also needs a separate boundary-complexity argument to obtain a regret bound.

Beyond polynomials

The other part of the paper considers equations of the form ⟨α, F(x)⟩ = 0. Here F can be a nonpolynomial feature curve, so degree alone is no longer the right language.

For a nonzero feature curve, multiplying F(x) by a nonzero scalar does not change the zero condition. The direction of F is therefore the relevant geometric object, leading to a projective formulation.

The coefficient-body theorem expresses the worst-case hitting constant through section-averaged projective incidence. For cube-supported coefficients, the resulting comparison avoids the previous √N dimension loss.

This geometric result and the ordered-root criterion address different coefficient models. The latter is particularly useful when a structured random process produces a singular law, as in the shared-offset example.

For an application, the remaining work is to identify the actual transition equations, check the distribution assumptions, and control how many boundaries can occur. A root bound is one ingredient of the learning guarantee.
