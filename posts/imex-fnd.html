IMEX-FND: learning what text and images contribute to a verdict

Zijun Wang · Multimodal learning · 7 min read

The route-then-decompose design behind IMEX-FND: modality replacement objectives, conflict-subset results, and the limits of expert-weight explanations.

One image, several possible roles

Consider a genuine photograph attached to a false claim about a different event. The text can be fluent and the image unaltered. The misleading part is the relationship between them.

In another post, the image may be decisive because it has been manipulated. In a third, text and image may repeat the same information. These are illustrative cases, but they motivate different ways of using the two modalities.

IMEX-FND asks how a detector can adapt to these changing relationships while exposing something more useful than a single fusion score. Our design combines instance-level routing with explicit interaction experts.

Route first, then model interactions

Routing adapts each representation to the post

Frozen encoders extract text, image, and CLIP-grounded cross-modal features. These signals carry different information and are projected into a common downstream feature space.

The Multi-Modal Expert Gateway (MMEG) uses sample-dependent gates to mix specialist and shared experts. Its outputs are three refined streams: text, image, and fusion.

The shared experts provide a route for common structure, while specialists retain modality-specific information. A gate can therefore choose a different mixture for a text-led post and an image-led post.

Interaction experts assign different jobs to the joint input

The Interaction-aware Multi-Modal Expert Fusion module (IMEF) takes the three streams together. Five experts represent text uniqueness, image uniqueness, fusion uniqueness, synergy, and redundancy.

zint = Σj=1…5 αj(z) Ej(z),   z = [t; v; m]

All five experts see the joint input. Their names alone cannot make them specialize. The central training question is how to give those names operational meaning.

How the experts learn distinct jobs

During training, we replace one routed stream with noise while keeping the others fixed. We compare each expert’s response to the original input and to these perturbed versions.

Intended behaviour under training-time stream replacement, based on Section 3.5.
ExpertDesired response
Text / image / fusion uniquenessChange most when its own stream is replaced; remain closer when another stream is replaced.
SynergyChange when any required stream is replaced.
RedundancyRemain stable when one stream is replaced, because overlapping evidence remains.

For a uniqueness expert, a triplet-margin objective keeps the original output closer to irrelevant-stream replacements than to replacement of its own stream. Synergy and redundancy use complementary similarity objectives.

This is weak supervision for interaction structure. Replacing a feature stream is a controlled operation inside the model; it does not require a human annotation for the interaction type of every post.

The replacement passes operate on the lightweight interaction module and are disabled at inference. Auxiliary text, image, and fusion heads also supply direct prediction signals during training.

A hybrid fusion path balances the interaction representation with a conventional fused representation. This preserves another route to useful predictive information when the expert decomposition is imperfect.

Replacement objectives and hybrid fusion: Sections 3.5–3.6.

Where routing helps most

The overall ablations help distinguish the jobs of the two stages. Removing interaction decomposition causes the largest single-component Macro-F1 drop on each dataset. Removing routing is the next largest drop.

The conflict subset is more revealing than the average

Using the auxiliary text-only and image-only heads, we divide posts according to whether their predictions agree. The disagreement subset is a useful place to inspect a model intended to adapt its reliance on each modality.

On Weibo, the routing gap is 3.4 Macro-F1 points on conflict posts and 0.6 on agreement posts. The corresponding conflict gaps are 2.8 points on Weibo-21 and 3.1 on Gossip.

This suggests that routing is particularly useful when the unimodal signals compete. The split is defined by model predictions, so it is a diagnostic proxy rather than an independently labelled causal test of modality importance.

Accuracy can hide which class remains difficult

On Gossip, the full model has 0.908 accuracy and 0.872 Macro-F1. Fake-class F1 is 0.805, while real-class F1 is 0.939. Reporting both classes makes the remaining difficulty visible.

The fusion-weight sweep also gives a useful caution. Performance generally improves up to a moderate interaction weight, then saturates or decreases. The reported configuration uses λ = 0.3.

I read that as evidence for balancing the two paths. The results do not support assuming that greater reliance on the interaction experts must always improve a detector.

My contribution

My work covered multimodal representation construction, instance-level expert routing, interaction-aware training, and the main and ablation experiments on Weibo, Weibo-21, and Gossip.

A common interface for heterogeneous features

I built the feature pipeline using a BERT-style text encoder, an MAE-pretrained ViT-B/16 image encoder, and CLIP ViT-B/16. The downstream modules needed compatible representations while retaining different encoder signals.

This required treating token-level features, pooled routing summaries, and cross-modal embeddings as different inputs with different purposes. Reducing everything to one pooled vector would remove information used by the expert gateway.

Routing and interaction specialization

I worked on the instance-level routing design and the five-expert interaction decomposition. The goal was to adapt the model’s use of evidence for each post and make the resulting interaction weights inspectable.

I worked on modality replacement and the associated training objectives, including the triplet uniqueness loss, synergy loss, and auxiliary heads. These signals connect an expert’s assigned role to measurable behaviour under perturbation.

Controlled comparisons

I carried out the main evaluations and component ablations across the three benchmarks. Comparing the complete model with routing, interaction, and training-signal removals helped separate the contributions of the design choices.

The key question for this evaluation was where a component helped. Aggregate scores describe the overall result; the agreement–conflict split gives a more specific account of when adaptive routing matters.

How much an expert weight explains

The interaction weights expose the model’s allocation across experts. For example, Table 4 reports a claim–photo mismatch with a synergy weight of 0.62. Such cases make the learned mechanism easier to inspect.

A large synergy weight does not, by itself, prove that the real-world misinformation mechanism was synergistic. The weights belong to a learned parameterization, and different experts may still encode overlapping information.

There is also a gap between replacing an internal stream with noise and removing evidence from a real post. The fusion stream already contains cross-modal information, so keeping it fixed does not erase every trace of a replaced modality.

For a follow-up study, I would test whether attributions remain stable across random seeds and whether removing the claimed decisive evidence changes predictions as expected. Those tests would assess explanation faithfulness more directly.

The current experiments support predictive gains and useful internal diagnostics. The next explanation question is whether those diagnostics reliably track the evidence that controls a decision.
