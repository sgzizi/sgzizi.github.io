EVENODD: what a recovery-time benchmark needs to check

Zijun Wang · Coding theory · 3 min read

A technical note on double-disk erasure recovery, the rank condition behind XOR parity, and fair comparisons of correctness, latency, and I/O.

Read the paper Technical note

In this article

  1. Start with the recovery guarantee
  2. Why a second parity column matters
  3. Measure correct recovery before speed

Start with the recovery guarantee

Suppose a decoder returns quickly after three disks fail. Before comparing its latency with another decoder, we need to establish whether it recovered the missing data at all.

Classical EVENODD is a double-erasure array code. It adds two redundant columns and uses XOR-based row and diagonal parity to recover any two erased columns under the construction’s assumptions.

The guarantee concerns known erasure locations. It should not be silently extended to arbitrary numbers of failed columns or to unknown corruption, which changes what the decoder must infer.

The original construction: Blaum, Brady, Bruck, and Menon, 1995.

Why a second parity column matters

With one missing value in a row, XOR parity gives a direct solution: XOR the surviving values with the stored row parity.

Dmissing = Prow ⊕ D₁ ⊕ D₂ ⊕ ⋯

With two missing values, the same equation determines only their XOR. Many pairs produce that value. The diagonal constraints supply additional equations across rows.

EVENODD’s diagonal construction includes an adjustment term; it is not simply a second copy of row parity with a different traversal order. The structure must make the erased variables uniquely recoverable.

At bit level, recovery can be viewed as a linear system over GF(2). For an erased-variable vector e, surviving data determine a right-hand side b:

AEe = b   over GF(2)

Unique recovery requires the columns of AE to be linearly independent. Counting inexpensive operations says little about whether that condition holds for the failure pattern being tested.

Measure correct recovery before speed

A useful comparison fixes the data size, redundancy budget, and erasure pattern. Otherwise, a method with more redundancy or less missing data may appear faster for an unrelated reason.

I would structure a benchmark around the following checks:

  1. Encode the same source data and record the amount of redundant storage.
  2. Erase a specified set of columns, including every single- and double-column pattern for the chosen array size.
  3. Decode and compare every recovered bit with the original data.
  4. Report latency and bytes read for successful recovery, alongside failed or unrecoverable cases.
  5. Separate encoding, update, and reconstruction costs instead of collapsing them into one runtime.

For a toy array, exhaustive input and erasure checks can expose indexing mistakes. Larger arrays need randomized payloads, repeated measurements, and explicit reporting of the failure patterns sampled.

Tests beyond two erased columns can be useful stress tests. Their outcomes need their own conditions; they cannot establish a general recovery guarantee beyond the redundancy budget.

CPU operations and data movement should also be recorded separately. Fewer XORs need not yield less I/O, and the bottleneck can change with block size and the storage system.

My work on parity computation

My earlier work examined parity-calculation approaches and their recovery cost in storage-failure settings. I analysed the parity and reconstruction mechanisms and used simulations to compare complexity and execution efficiency.

The associated article compares XOR-based, Reed–Solomon, and matrix-based approaches. This note examines that comparison: correctness conditions and redundancy must be established before comparing timings.

For future implementations, I would keep the correctness check outside the timed region while requiring every timed output to pass it. Unrecoverable cases should be recorded as such, rather than being rewarded for returning quickly.
